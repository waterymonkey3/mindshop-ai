MindShop AI
an AI store manager
for online shops

A shop owner knows the product, not online marketing. MindShop AI is an AI agent the owner talks to — a chat window instead of a dashboard, plain language instead of forms. Nothing to install, nothing to learn. It learns one specific shop: its live catalogue, its rules, its own publishing history. Then it does the work through the shop's real APIs — writes and publishes content, creates the products the catalogue is missing, rewrites weak listings, translates them, audits the site and advises on the business. It is built for e-commerce sites, not for one industry — the pilot happens to be a manufacturer — and the same engine is meant for marketplace and storefront platforms next. Nothing goes live without the owner's word in the default mode, and every result is read back from the live site.

Demo — 4:54, one take

Six requests typed into one chat, in the order they happened. The agent calls the pilot store's own admin APIs — one for content publishing, one for product management — and reads every result back from the live site, so the counts you see are real.

Using it is just talking

  • It reaches you on the platforms you already use. Telegram today — where a Russian seller already spends the day — and the same agent core plugs into the other major messaging platforms, so the shop can be run from wherever the owner and the team actually are, on whatever device is at hand.
  • Nothing to adopt, nothing to learn. You type a request in plain language and the work gets done. There is no new app to install and no admin panel to sit through — the owners this is built for have no marketing team, but every one of them already types messages on a phone.
  • The owner speaks business, the agent does the rest. You say "those older products aren't good enough" — it reads the catalogue, audits it, reports what it found, and does what you approve. No settings screen, no fields to fill in, no training to sit through.

What it actually did, request by request

  1. Wrote and published an industry article. It drafted the piece from Russian marketplace sources, then checked its own published articles for overlap first — "Checked against your live articles: no overlap." — and stopped to ask permission before publishing. On approval it published through the API and verified on the live site: news total is now 7.
  2. Created a product the catalogue was missing. It read the live catalogue first (nine products), found a real gap — no packaging paper at all — and proposed one product: category, price, MOQ, specifications and a photo it had verified loads on the site. It then waited for an answer. After "Create it!": id 14, and product total is now 10.
  3. Audited the rest of the catalogue — and stopped at the line it would not cross. Told that the older products were not good enough, it checked them one by one and reported instead of editing: repeated specifications on six products, and names that read like a category rather than a product. "Both are your call."
  4. Rewrote two listings once the owner pointed at them. On "write 8 and 9" it brought two thin descriptions back — 16 → 444 and 20 → 404 characters — in a fixed five-section structure, and verified both on the live site.
  5. Translated the same two products into English — and warned first. It wrote the English version and flagged the consequence before touching the site: "your catalogue would then be part-Chinese, part-English." Then it replaced both and checked them live.
  6. Audited the website and the business — read-only. Asked for advice, it ran an audit that does not touch the code and answered from the site's own weak points instead of generic advice: the sitemap covers three URLs out of twenty, and search engines can't see the product pages. It also named the search engine that matters here: "Google sometimes executes JavaScript. Yandex doesn't — and Yandex is your market." Then, at company level, it opened with what it cannot see, and named the wedge it would bet on: FBS and marking compliance.

Why this is an agent, not a writing tool

It remembers one shop — and only that shop

Before acting it reads the shop's live catalogue and its own published articles, so decisions are grounded in this business rather than in generic best practice. The shop's own rules live in the agent's working files — for example Chinese and English listings and articles must contain no Cyrillic characters. This knowledge is accumulated per shop: a second shop means teaching it a second shop, not shipping different software.

It rewrites its own playbook

After finishing the work — without being asked — it reviews what it just did and patches its own procedure file, which is visible in the demo. The same mistake should not have to be explained twice.

Permission gates, on by default

Out of the box, publishing, creating and rewriting stop at a question first; that is the mode you see in the demo, and what it is unsure about is reported as “your call”. The same workflows also run unattended: once the owner trusts the pattern, generation goes live with no approval step, and the agent's scheduler triggers the workflows on a timer — the shop owner does nothing at all.

It says where its knowledge stops

Asked for advice on the company, it opens by stating what it cannot see — "I can't see your finances, team or capacity" — and answers only from what it can actually read. It refuses to invent numbers. Where mistakes cost real orders, that restraint matters more than raw speed.

What it is built on

MindShop AI is built on a mature, MIT-licensed open-source agent architecture. That gives the product, from day one, capabilities that would otherwise take months to build — persistent memory across sessions, self-editing procedures, tool permissions, scheduled runs. What we build — and where all the engineering goes — is the layer specific to the shop: its knowledge, its rules, its workflows.

Where we are

  • Two workflows run on the live pilot store today: automated collection, writing and publication of industry news (Chinese and Russian sources), and competitor product research → product creation and optimisation. Both act through the site's admin APIs.
  • Approval mode and full-auto are both built. The demo shows the approval mode, because that is the behaviour a shop owner has to be able to trust first. With the gate switched off, the same workflows publish with no human in the loop, and the agent's scheduler then triggers them on a timer — both modes are available today, and switching between them is a setting.
  • The engine is shop-agnostic. It works on a live site through its APIs, so the next targets are marketplace seller APIs and hosted storefront platforms — the same engine pointed at a different backend, not a different product. Multi-shop onboarding and the customer-service workflow (answering buyers from the same knowledge base) are still ahead of us.
  • No commercial revenue yet. Everything described above runs today on a live commercial site — the accelerator is the step that turns it into a business.
