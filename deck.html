MindShop AIBackground

A shop owner knows the product — not online marketing

Millions of small sellers on marketplaces and on their own sites can make the product. Almost none of them can do the content, the catalogue work and the SEO — and none of them can hire for it.

01
Agencies cost too much
At small-merchant scale, outsourcing content and SEO does not pay back. The budget that would make it work is the budget a small shop does not have.
02
Generic AI writes generic text
Tools like Jasper or Copy.ai produce plausible copy that knows nothing about the business — because they were never given the business.
03
Dashboards report, they don't act
Marketplace analytics show data and leave every task to the owner. Knowledge is not the bottleneck; execution is.

So good small shops stay invisible — while competitors with a marketing team win.

MindShop AISolution

Four behaviours a prompt cannot give you

01
It remembers one shop
Before acting it reads the live catalogue and its own published articles. The shop's rules live in its working files — e.g. Chinese and English listings and articles must contain no Cyrillic characters. Knowledge accumulates per shop: a second shop means teaching it a second shop, not shipping different software.
02
It rewrites its own playbook
After finishing the work — unprompted — it reviews what it just did and patches its own procedure file. The same mistake should not have to be explained twice.
03
Permission gates, by default
Publishing, creating and rewriting stop at a question first. Anything it is unsure about is reported as "your call" — never changed silently behind the owner's back.
04
It says where its knowledge stops
Asked about the company, it opens with "I can't see your finances, team or capacity" — and answers only from what it can read. It refuses to invent numbers.

MindShop AISolution

Reachable on the platforms you already use

The shop is operated from a messenger — Telegram first, with the same agent core behind the other major messaging platforms.

01
It comes to where the owner already is
Telegram today, where a Russian seller already spends the day. The same agent core connects to the other major messaging platforms, so the shop can be run from wherever the owner and the team actually are — one agent, many doors.
02
Nothing to adopt, nothing to learn
You type a request in plain language and the work gets done. No new app to install and no admin panel to sit through — the owners this is built for have no marketing team, but every one of them already types messages on a phone.
03
Hands-off whenever you want it
The demo shows the approval mode — every publish stops for a yes/no. Switch the gate off and the same workflows publish unattended, triggered by the agent's scheduler on a timer. Both modes are built in today.

You speak business — "those older products aren't good enough" — and the agent does the rest.

It runs on a live commercial site today

  • Two workflows are live today: news collection, writing and publication (Chinese and Russian sources) and competitor research → product creation and optimisation. Both act through the site's own admin APIs.
  • Both run in two modes: approval-first, which the demo shows, and fully unattended, where generation goes live with no approval step and the agent's scheduler triggers it on a timer.
  • The pilot is one real factory — a normal small manufacturer, operating normally while the agent works on its live site.
  • Where it stops today: no commercial revenue yet, no second shop onboarded, and the customer-service workflow is planned rather than built.

MindShop AIVision

Same engine, next backends

The core · open source (MIT)
Built on a mature, MIT-licensed open-source agent architecture. The product starts with capabilities that would otherwise take months to build — persistent memory, self-editing procedures, tool permissions, scheduled runs. All of the engineering then goes into the layer that is specific to the shop.
The product
On top of that core we build the shop-specific layer: its knowledge and rules, the content and catalogue workflows, the operating procedures, and the API integrations that let the agent act on a real shop instead of talking about one.
What's next
Per shop, not per industry. Multi-shop onboarding, the customer-service workflow, and the same engine pointed at marketplace seller APIs and hosted storefronts — a different backend, not a different product.
